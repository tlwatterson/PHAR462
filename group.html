The Group Project Meltdown

A demonstration case for learning how branching scenarios work.

You are working on a group presentation worth 30% of your course grade. The presentation is tomorrow morning. At 9:47 PM your phone lights up. A message appears in the group chat:

"Sorry everyone. I don't think I can finish my section."

No explanation. No follow-up. No response. The rest of the group immediately starts texting.

What do you do?

Several students immediately respond.

"Unbelievable."
"Seriously?"
"Why does this always happen?"

The conversation becomes increasingly frustrated. Ten minutes later the group member responds:

"I'm at the emergency vet."
"My dog stopped breathing."

What now?

You respond:

"Is everything okay?"

Several minutes later you receive a reply.

"I'm at the emergency animal hospital."
"I don't know what's going to happen."

The tone of the group chat changes immediately. People begin discussing solutions.

What do you do?

You decide not to make assumptions. Instead, you post:

"Let's come up with some backup options while we wait to hear more."

The group starts discussing contingency plans. A few minutes later the group member explains the emergency vet situation. Fortunately, the group already has some options prepared.

What now?

Ending: Recovery

The group apologizes for making assumptions. Work is redistributed. The presentation goes well. Relationships within the team recover.

Lesson: Sometimes the information we are missing matters more than the information we have.

Ending: Group Chat Disaster

The project is completed. However, screenshots of the conversation are later shared with the teammate. Trust within the group drops dramatically. The presentation succeeds. The team relationship does not.

Lesson: Assumptions can continue to cause harm even after new information emerges.

Ending: Team-Based Solution

Everyone contributes a little extra work. Responsibilities are redistributed. The presentation succeeds and the workload feels manageable.

Lesson: Good problem solving often starts with understanding the situation.

Ending: Support First

An hour later the teammate sends completed slides from the waiting room. The original plan remains mostly intact. The teammate later tells the group:

"Thanks for checking on me first."

Lesson: Not every problem requires immediate action. Sometimes understanding comes first.

Ending: Organized Under Pressure

By the time the group learns what happened, multiple backup plans already exist. The team adapts quickly. Everyone feels less stressed.

Lesson: Preparation creates flexibility.

Ending: Creative Problem Solving

The teammate records a short video presentation from the waiting room. The next day the audience never realizes there was a crisis. The presentation runs smoothly.

Lesson: Sometimes the best solution is one you didn't initially consider.

Debrief

  1. At what point did you think you knew what was happening?
  2. What assumptions did you make?
  3. Which decision generated the most information?
  4. Was there a single best path?
  5. How does this relate to patient care?

Key Takeaway:

People often create explanations before they have complete information. In healthcare, asking questions and gathering information can completely change how a situation is understood.
